\section{Implementation and Evaluation}
\label{sec:benchmark}

For an empirical evaluation, we implemented the two masked-lookup modulus-conversion protocols by realizing $\func_\abb$ for a dishonest majority, using the SPDZ2k protocol~\cite{C:CDESX18}, i.e., authenticated additive secret sharing.
This places our numbers under the same security model as Fhenix~\cite{CCS:ZZLP25}, which we compare against and which is likewise dishonest-majority.
Following Fhenix, we implemented the offline and online parts separately:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item
        \emph{Offline phase.}
        Implemented in MP-SPDZ~\cite{EPRINT:Keller20}.
        In this phase all correlated randomness specific to a conversion is prepared: the mask together with the correction table, by \cref{protocol:modconv1-preproc} and \cref{protocol:modconv2-preproc} respectively.
    \item
        \emph{Online phase.}
        Also in MP-SPDZ, so that both phases are measured by the same instrumentation and over the same wire protocol.
        Mirroring Fhenix, whose precomputed gates are loaded into memory before timing starts, the correlated randomness is written to disk beforehand and read by the parties at run time, so that the timer covers the online phase and nothing else.
        We stress that this material is produced by a trusted dealer and its generation is therefore \emph{not} timed here; what it costs to produce securely is exactly what \cref{sec:benchmark-preproc} measures, with no dealer.
        The separation is not cosmetic: MP-SPDZ otherwise generates correlated randomness lazily, inside the timed region, and bills that generation to the online timer.
        Left unattended this inflated our measured online communication from $96$ bytes per decryption to $6405$, and \cref{sec:benchmark-caveats} records how the figures reported here are guarded against it.
\end{enumerate}

We convert from $q = 2^{63}$ to $t = 2^{64}$, the smallest nontrivial 2-power lift, chosen so that $q \mid t$ and \cref{remark:modconv1-divisible,remark:modconv2-divisible} both apply: $\shared{m}_q$ is never stored, following from any one of the corrections by a $\DivModConv$ command at no cost.
Programs are compiled for the ring $\ints_{2^{64}}$, so the machine ring is exactly $t$ and no slack bits are needed anywhere.
We use the same party counts $N \in \{4, 8, 16\}$ as Fhenix for the online phase, and $N = 2$ for the preprocessing.
Since $q$ and $\beta$ are 2-powers, the preprocessing of \cref{protocol:modconv1-preproc} loses its comparison, its acceptance test and its rejection loop, as the text following that protocol records; it then consists of $63$ shared random bits and local linear combinations.
For $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$, whose $\beta$ must satisfy $\beta \ge N+1$, we take the smallest 2-power above that floor, which keeps the precondition at $x \le q/2$ for every $N$, uniformly with $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ at $\beta = 2$.

Every run checks its opened plaintext against the value computed in the clear, without a further opening.

\subsection{Decryption}
\label{sec:benchmark-online}

We measure the protocols as the decryption step they are meant to serve, not as a bare conversion.
Before the timer starts we sample a small secret key $\shared{\bm{s}} \in \ints_t^n$ and a public ciphertext $\bm{c} \in \ints_t^n$, and resample until $\langle \bm{c}, \bm{s}\rangle \bmod q$ meets the precondition of the conversion protocol;
accepting an instance is exactly the statement that the underlying error term lies in range.
We take $n = 1024$, matching the \gls{lwe} parameters $(n, q, p) = (1024, 2^{64}, 2)$ of \cite{CCS:ZZLP25}; as there, this is plain \gls{lwe} over $\ints_q$ rather than ring-\gls{lwe}, the secret key being a vector over $\ints_q$ and the first step of decryption an inner product.
The timed part is then
\[
    \shared{x}_t \coloneq \langle \bm{c}, \shared{\bm{s}} \rangle,
    \qquad
    \shared{x \bmod q}_q \gets \DivModConv(\shared{x}_t),
    \qquad
    \shared{x \bmod q}_t \gets \ModConv(\shared{x \bmod q}_q),
\]
followed by opening $\shared{m}_t \coloneq \shared{x}_t - \shared{x \bmod q}_t$, which yields $m = q \cdot \mathrm{bit}_{63}(x)$, the one-bit plaintext.
The $\DivModConv$ is free and is not even an instruction: the machine ring is $t$ and $q \mid t$, so the reduction is the identity on the sharing.

This costs two openings, one more than the conversion alone, and that extra one is deliberate.
The inner product and the final subtraction are local operations, but under SPDZ2k they still act on the MAC, and that cost is only realized when the run opens something;
timing the conversion in isolation would leave it out.
The inner product itself turns out to cost no communication whatever the dimension --- we measure the same five rounds and $0.36$ kB per opening at $n = 1$, $256$ and $1024$ --- since a linear combination and its MAC update are both local, so $n$ buys local computation only.
That local computation is not negligible, however: holding everything else fixed, the timed phase takes $0.014$~s at $n = 1$ against $0.037$~s at $n = 1024$, so the inner product is about $62\%$ of the online time.
It is therefore the dimension, and not the conversion, that governs how much a party's own cores matter to throughput.

\Cref{tab:benchmark-online} reports the result.
The measured round counts are MP-SPDZ's own, which count one round per polling iteration of its socket exchange rather than per network round-trip; the protocol-level figure is the two openings, against the three sequential openings of Fhenix's online phase.

\subsection{Preprocessing}
\label{sec:benchmark-preproc}

\Cref{tab:benchmark-preproc} reports the cost of preparing the material for one conversion, at $N = 2$ and under an emulated $1$~ms ping time and $1$~Gbit/sec bandwidth.

The preprocessing is where the two protocols separate.
$\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ issues no secure multiplication at all, but it needs $63$ shared random bits, and it is those bits that dominate its cost entirely.
$\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$ instead has each party contribute its own mask, so it consumes no shared randomness and only the $(N-1)\beta^2 = 16$ multiplications of its convolution, and comes out an order of magnitude cheaper on both counts.
Neither figure is dominated by the protocol: \cref{tab:benchmark-preproc-cmp} separates the two contributions.
That comparison is not like for like, however, and \cref{sec:benchmark-caveats} says why.
It is worth noting where $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$'s cost actually sits: a shared random bit is not free in SPDZ2k, which realizes it by the square-root trick, drawing on Beaver triples generated over an auxiliary ring.
The protocol issues no $\Mul$ command, as \cref{tab:modconv} records, but the $\RandomBit$ commands it does issue are what the measurement is made of, so that is where an optimization of this preprocessing would have to bite.

Two things should be read off \cref{tab:benchmark-preproc-cmp}, and a third should not.

The robust comparison is $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$ against \cite{CCS:ZZLP25}: both spend their preprocessing on secure multiplications, so the ratio is one of counts at a common price and does not depend on how triples are produced.
Sixteen against $2478$ is a factor of $155$, and the measured overhead agrees, $1.55$~MB against $79.29$ per thousand.
Their own estimate of what they exclude, ``approximately $4.5$~MB of communication per decryption'', is reproduced by the first column and is $145$ times $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$'s $0.031$.

The overhead column on its own is not the result, and for $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ it is actively misleading.
Once its random bits are supplied there is nothing left to do --- the corrections are a local linear combination, and we measure zero rounds and zero bytes --- so reading $0$ against $79.29$~MB would be reading the consequence of having excluded the only thing the protocol does.
What the zero says precisely is that $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$'s preprocessing \emph{is} a request for $63$ random bits, with no structure on top; whether that is cheap is entirely the question of what a random bit costs.

That price is the third thing, and it must be applied to both sides at once.
We measure $21.9$~kB for an authenticated random bit in SPDZ2k, which realizes it through square pairs and hence through triples, and $17.4$~kB for a triple.
Charging $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$'s bits at our measured price while charging \cite{CCS:ZZLP25}'s triples at the Multipars figure they quote --- a ninefold cheaper basis --- would put the two within a factor of four; the columns above avoid that.
On either basis consistently applied the ordering is the same, $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ between $31$ and $40$ times below and $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$ around $150$, and the spread between the two bases is a statement about triple generation rather than about either protocol.
What no price can move is the count: $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ issues no secure multiplication at all, as \cref{tab:modconv} records.

Put in the units of \cref{tab:benchmark-preproc}, their gate preprocessing for one thousand decryptions, run in MP-SPDZ at the triple cost we measure there, would come to some $41$~GB and $980$ seconds, against $1.31$~GB and $37.4$~s for $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ and $0.27$~GB and $6.37$~s for $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$.
This is why the two conventions must not be crossed: our end-to-end $37.4$~s set against their generation-free $1.35$~s reads as a defeat by a factor of $27$, and is an artefact of comparing a total with a remainder.

The same caution applies to \cref{tab:benchmark-preproc}, whose figures include the generation and are therefore dominated by it.
Generating $63\,000$ shared random bits in SPDZ2k and doing nothing else costs $1272.76$~MB; the complete preprocessing of $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ for the same one thousand conversions costs $1344$~MB.
The protocol's own contribution --- the corrections, the one-hot vector and the linear combinations --- is thus under $6\%$, and the rest is the price SPDZ2k charges for an authenticated random bit, about $20.2$~kB each.
Any protocol requiring $63$ such bits per conversion would pay the same, and a bespoke implementation would not pay it at all.
The defensible cost of this preprocessing is therefore the count of primitives it issues --- $63$ $\RandomBit$ commands and no $\Mul$ for $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$, $(N-1)\beta^2$ $\Mul$ commands and no $\RandomBit$ for $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$ --- with the megabytes read as a property of the framework rather than of the protocol.

\subsection{Network Conditions}
\label{sec:benchmark-network}

Fhenix emulates its network with \texttt{tc}, crossing ping times $\{1, 10, 100\}\,\mathrm{ms}$ with bandwidths $\{100\,\mathrm{Mbit/s}, 1\,\mathrm{Gbit/s}\}$, and \cref{tab:benchmark-latency} reports our figures over that same grid at $1$~Gbit/sec.
All parties run on one host and communicate over loopback, as Fhenix's own harness does; the emulated delay is therefore set to half the nominal ping time, since a loopback packet crosses the interface once in each direction.
Every run is preceded by a check that \texttt{netem} is in fact attached and by a measurement of the resulting loopback round-trip time, and the harness refuses to run unshaped rather than produce rows carrying a ping label they did not experience.
That guard exists because an earlier sweep did produce such rows: the emulation silently failed to attach, and the near-identical times at nominal $1$, $10$ and $100$~ms were the only sign.
The columns of \cref{tab:benchmark-latency} scale with the ping time as they should, which is what the guard is there to ensure.

One asymmetry should be recorded, because it runs against us.
Fhenix's single-ciphertext latency measurements are taken with a delay but no bandwidth limit at all, whereas every figure we report is additionally capped at $1$~Gbit/sec on a loopback shared by all $N$ parties.
The comparison in \cref{sec:benchmark-comparison} is therefore conservative in their favour.

\subsection{Throughput and Comparison with Fhenix}
\label{sec:benchmark-comparison}

\Cref{tab:benchmark-online,tab:benchmark-latency} time one decryption at a time.
Throughput is a different question, and it is the one \cite[Table~1]{CCS:ZZLP25} answers, so we measure it the same way: batched decryption at $N = 4$ under a $1$~ms ping time and $1$~Gbit/sec bandwidth.

The property that decides this comparison is that \emph{our round complexity does not depend on the batch size}.
The batch is vectorized rather than looped, so a thousand decryptions issue the same two opening instructions as one; we measure $11$ rounds and $96$ bytes per decryption, unchanged from $1000$ to $10\,000$ decryptions per batch.
A larger batch therefore dilutes a fixed network cost rather than paying it again, and that, rather than any local optimization, is what the figures below reflect.

\Cref{tab:benchmark-throughput} sets our grid against theirs, and it does not resolve into a single ratio.
The two implementations are limited by different things, and which one wins is decided by which limit binds.

At $1$~Gbit/sec and a $1$~ms ping we are ahead at $N = 4$, $70\,589$ against $64\,319$, and behind at $8$ and $16$ parties.
As the ping time rises, that column collapses for us and barely moves for them: at $100$~ms and $N = 4$ they still achieve $30\,715$ where we manage $4\,238$.
The reason is visible in \cref{tab:benchmark-latency} and in their own description of their harness.
Our batch is one job whose eleven measured rounds are serial, so at $100$~ms it spends over a second waiting whatever the batch contains, while their published logs show ten jobs of $6500$ ciphertexts per party running concurrently, which hides the round trips behind each other.
Vectorizing a batch removes the \emph{number} of rounds; it does not overlap them with anything, and pipelining does.

At $100$~Mbit/sec the ordering reverses almost everywhere, by as much as $2.45\times$ at $N = 4$ and $1$~ms and $2.40\times$ at $N = 4$ and $100$~ms.
Here it is bandwidth that binds rather than latency, and bandwidth is where the protocol shows: we measure $101$ bytes per decryption per party at $N = 4$, growing as $N-1$, against the $1.42$~kB per decryption implied by their own $100$~Mbit figures at the same party count.
The gap narrows at $16$ parties, where our communication grows as $N(N-1)$ in aggregate and theirs more slowly, and the two are within five percent.

Two things follow for the reading of these tables, and both are ours to concede.
The first is that our $1$~Gbit high-ping cells are limited by the batch and not by the protocol: at $100$~ms the round cost is fixed per batch, so the batch of $3000$ used here --- chosen to fit the offline data on disk --- would improve roughly threefold at $10\,000$, and still not reach them, because what separates us there is pipelining.
The second is that five threads per party helps only where the host is the constraint, at $1$~ms, and hurts everywhere else: our threading splits one batch across tapes that each open separately, taking the round count from eleven to fifty-five, which at $100$~ms costs far more than the arithmetic it parallelises.
Their concurrency is of independent jobs and does not pay that.
The winning thread count is given in each cell for that reason.

The honest summary is therefore not a ratio but a characterisation.
Ours moves less data and needs fewer openings; theirs tolerates latency better because it pipelines.
On a fast link with high latency they win, on a constrained link we win.
The protocol-level quantities are ours regardless of which implementation is measured --- two openings against three, and $101$ bytes per decryption per party against $1.42$~kB --- but as the discussion of round trips below records, the first of those two is not what the latency figures currently reflect.

Latency admits a cleaner comparison than throughput, because it is governed by round trips rather than by how fast a party computes or how deeply it pipelines.
\Cref{tab:benchmark-latency} gives ours beside theirs.
At $N = 4$ and a $1$~ms ping we are the faster, $6.79$~ms against $8.48$.

It is worth being exact about where that comes from, because the openings alone do not explain it.
Fitting the measured times against the ping time gives the number of round trips actually spent: $6.00$ for $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ and $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$ alike, at every party count, against $5.56$ for \cite{CCS:ZZLP25} --- which is its own stated ``three sequential public openings, and in this implementation, two more rounds of authenticity checks''.
So we spend \emph{more} round trips than they do, not fewer, despite issuing two openings against their three, and are ahead at this point only on the constant.
The reason is that SPDZ2k checks the MAC of an opened value before that value may influence a further secret computation, and our second opening consumes the first: the index $\alpha$ that selects the correction is derived from the opened $y$.
Two openings that do not depend on each other cost what one costs --- we measure five of MP-SPDZ's rounds for either --- while two that do cost twice that, ten.
Each of ours is therefore one round trip to reconstruct and two to check, where \cite{CCS:ZZLP25} opens three times and checks once at the end.

The protocol-level advantage is real and the implementation spends it.
Deferring the first check to the end is sound in the usual sense --- the opened $y$ is masked and reveals nothing, a forged share would select a wrong correction, and the final check would catch it before any output is released --- and would bring us to four round trips against their five.
We have not implemented it, and the figures below are without it.
At $10$~ms we are behind by about a tenth at $N = 4$ and $8$, and at $100$~ms by a similar margin --- except at $16$ parties, where the ordering reverses and we measure $639.7$~ms against their $811.8$, a fifth faster, because their cost grows with the party count at high ping and ours does not.
Our $16$-party figures at $1$ and $10$~ms are the exception in the other direction and are not a comparison at all: they are limited by our host, as \cref{sec:benchmark-caveats} records.

\subsection{Caveats}
\label{sec:benchmark-caveats}

Four qualifications apply to the numbers above.

The first is that the preprocessing of $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$ takes each party's contribution through SPDZ2k's own authenticated $\Input$ rather than through the $\CheckedInput$ of \cref{step:modconv2-preproc:input,step:modconv2-preproc:range} and the consistency check of \cref{step:modconv2-preproc:cons-check};
only the hot-one check of \cref{step:modconv2-preproc:hot-one} is performed.
Its figures in \cref{tab:benchmark-preproc} therefore understate the protocol as specified, by an amount these measurements do not determine, and its advantage over $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ should not be read as a like-for-like comparison.
The preprocessing of $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ takes no input and is unaffected.

The second is that the online figures are taken with dealer-supplied preprocessing read from disk.
This is what makes them online figures at all --- MP-SPDZ would otherwise generate correlated randomness inside the timed region --- and it matches how Fhenix loads its precomputed gates before timing.
It also means the online and preprocessing sections must be read together: nothing in \cref{sec:benchmark-comparison} accounts for the cost of producing that material, which \cref{tab:benchmark-preproc} gives separately.
Every reported row was checked against the $96$ bytes per decryption that two openings of a $\ints_{2^{64}}$ share to $N-1$ peers must cost, which is the symptom by which a run that silently generated preprocessing can be told from one that did not; and against a plaintext computed in the clear, so that a batch which decrypted incorrectly can never be reported as a fast one.

The third is that all $N$ parties share a single $20$-core host, against the $128$ vCPUs on which \cite{CCS:ZZLP25} runs the same number of parties.
This is what limits our $16$-party latency at $1$ and $10$~ms: at a $1$~ms ping its five repetitions span a factor of two where $N = 4$ spans under two percent, and the column does not fall as the ping time falls, which a protocol-limited measurement would.
Those cells are upper bounds set by the machine, and a deployment with one host per party --- the setting the protocol is meant for --- would not have this behaviour.

The fourth is that we report a single batch rather than a pipeline.
Our throughput figures come from one batch of $3000$ decryptions whose eleven rounds are serial, where \cite{CCS:ZZLP25} runs ten concurrent jobs per party and therefore overlaps its round trips.
That is a property of our harness and not of $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv1}$ or $\Pi_\mathsf{ModConv2}$, which say nothing about how many decryptions may be in flight, and it accounts for the whole of the $1$~Gbit high-ping column.
We have not implemented pipelining and make no claim about what it would give.

Five things to decide.
(i) The deferred MAC check is the most valuable item here. We spend six round trips where they spend five, because SPDZ2k checks each opening before its value may feed the next and ours are dependent by construction --- I measured two independent openings costing five of MP-SPDZ's rounds and two dependent ones costing ten. Deferring the first check would give four against their five and improve the whole high-ping column by a third. Whether MP-SPDZ can be made to do it, I did not establish.
(ii) The $1$~Gbit high-ping cells used a batch of $3000$, forced by disk. Re-running the grid at $10\,000$ should improve them about threefold; it will not close the gap, but it removes an artefact of our own setup from a column we lose.
(iii) The $N = 16$ latency cells at $1$ and $10$~ms are host-limited and are the worst numbers in the paper. Re-run those two on a quiet larger machine, or drop the column and say why.
(iv) Pipelining is the whole of the $1$~Gbit high-ping gap and is a harness property, not a protocol one. \Cref{sec:benchmark-caveats} concedes it without measuring it.
(v) Name our machine; theirs is a \texttt{c7a.32xlarge} with $128$ vCPUs against our $20$, and the throughput table is not interpretable without both.
